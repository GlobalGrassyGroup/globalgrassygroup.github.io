Excitement was high on departure day. We collected our gear: backpacks, sampling tools, devices, and field notebooks. Our driver was ready to navigate the rough roads and, after a long, scenic journey from Lubumbashi in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), we finally reached the gates of Kundelungu National Park. From the moment we entered, we were struck by the wild beauty surrounding us. The vast stretches of wet savanna, with their colourful patches of floodplain forests, were fascinating (Figure 1).

Kundelungu National Park is a biodiversity hotspot. Established in 1970 to protect the region's unique ecosystems, including the cheetah and Burchell's zebra, the park is home to an impressive variety of flora and fauna. The Kundelungu High Plateau is the source of seven rivers, and the park reaches an altitude of about 1,700 m. 

Our team had the privilege of conducting a research project focused on plant diversity in the wet savannas at the springhead of the Lutshipuka River. This mission aimed to establish a system of permanent plots to study the biodiversity of the wet savannas (Figure 2).

Savannas are the dominant habitats and vary according to soil conditions. Dry savannas occur on elevated areas between two floodplain forests, while wet savannas are found near the floodplain forests. The lowest areas are dominated by sedges and several flowering species. These habitats remain permanently waterlogged and, in some sections, are connected to backwaters dominated by *Nymphaea* sp. The edges of these backwaters are particularly species-rich and harbour several rare and endemic species such as *Phyllanthus leonardianus*.

We chose the Global Grassy Group method to record species diversity, which allows sampling of large 50-by-50-meter plots (Figure 2). Nine permanent plots were established in Kundelungu National Park - the first GGG plots in the DRC - and arranged at regular intervals of 800 meters apart. Each plot was subdivided into 21 smaller subplots, from which each species was carefully collected (Figure 3) to create a duplicate specimen for deposition at the Herbarium of Lubumbashi. We recorded 119 vouchers and more than 130 species since the last survey of the area by Lisowski & Malaisse (1989). Among the notable findings was *Coleus pseudospeciosus* (Figure 4), an endemic species of springhead savannas known only from southeastern DRC and Zambia.

Our team has also begun investigating the intricate ecological interactions shaping these wet savannas. Pollinators are vital for plant reproduction and often serve as key indicators of ecosystem health. We found that some plant species appear to rely exclusively on specific pollinators; for instance, a particular grass species is primarily pollinated by the bee Apis mellifera, which, along with Xylocopa, is tolerant of the cold temperatures. This emphasizes the importance of an integrated approach that considers not only plant presence but also their environmental interactions.

Despite our enthusiasm and determination, the mission was not without its challenges. The overwhelming cold, day and night, and the flooded savannas made access to sites and work difficult. Some areas were marshy and hard to access, sometimes requiring detours or crossing gallery forests. And, our measuring tapes were not resistant when stretched!

Each day offered its share of unforgettable observations. We were lucky to catch sight of baboons moving nimbly through the trees and to hear the piercing cries of birds soaring over our campsite. Vital, our park ranger, enthusiastically shared his knowledge of the local fauna. He told us stories about bush pigs that hang around certain areas of the park and about antelopes that moved in herds in search of grazing grounds. These encounters reminded each of us of the crucial importance of preserving these natural habitats to ensure the survival not only of the plants we studied but also of the animal species that depend on them.

We emphasize the importance of using the data we have collected and to raise awareness among the public, park managers, and policymakers about the environmental issues facing springhead savannas. Thanks to this successful first mission, we can set a date for the next, which must take place before the rainy season begins (October-November) and use tape measures that won’t break when setting up plots.
